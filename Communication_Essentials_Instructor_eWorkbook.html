Full group

Using the Participant eWorkbook

Get participants into the eWorkbook and comfortable with the activity links and save controls before the first exercise.

2 min
Open Participant eWorkbook Participant section: Start Here

Instructor flow

  1. Post the participant eWorkbook link in chat and ask everyone to open it.
  2. Give participants a moment to confirm the workbook loads.
  3. Point out the left navigation, activity fields, and Download My Work control.
  4. Resolve access problems before the first activity.

Watch for

  • A participant whose workbook will not load.
  • Participants closing the workbook or assuming browser storage is a permanent backup.

Debrief / listen for

  • No formal debrief required.

Transition

Now that everyone has the workbook open, let’s begin.

Preview learner-facing instructions
  1. Open the participant eWorkbook from the link in chat in a separate browser tab.
  2. Keep the eWorkbook open throughout the session.
  3. Use the left navigation to move to each activity when directed.
  4. Before closing the workbook, use Download My Work so you keep a copy of your entries.
Individual, then full group

The 9 Dot Challenge

Show how assumptions affect how people interpret instructions and decide what options are available.

10 min
Open 9 Dot Challenge Open Participant eWorkbook Participant section: The 9 Dot Challenge

Instructor flow

  1. Launch or post the 9 Dot Challenge link.
  2. Give participants about 2 minutes to work independently without hints.
  3. Ask who found a solution and what made the task difficult.
  4. Debrief the unstated assumption that the lines must stay inside the square.
  5. Connect the assumption to workplace messages and handoffs.

Watch for

  • Staying inside the box.
  • Assuming rules that were never stated.
  • Moving too quickly to a solution.
  • Not asking clarifying questions.

Debrief / listen for

  • What assumption did you make about the dots?
  • How do similar assumptions show up in workplace messages or handoffs?

Transition

Communication works the same way. What seems obvious to one person may not be obvious to another.

Preview learner-facing instructions
  1. Open the 9 Dot Challenge from your eWorkbook.
  2. Connect all nine dots using four straight lines without lifting your pen or pointer.
  3. Try independently for about 2 minutes. Do not look for the solution until time is called.
  4. Be ready to share what assumption made the challenge difficult.
Breakout groups of 3–5

The Misrouted Train Incident

Apply the ripple-effect concept by identifying where communication broke down and what consequences followed.

18 min
Open Participant eWorkbook Participant section: The Misrouted Train Incident

Instructor flow

  1. Frame this as a case analysis, not a right-answer quiz.
  2. Place participants in groups of three to five.
  3. Give groups 10 minutes to discuss all four questions.
  4. Keep about 8 minutes for report-out and debrief.
  5. Ask each group for one breakdown and one consequence so the first group does not answer everything.

Watch for

  • No documented communication plan.
  • Unclear ownership.
  • Missing confirmation or follow-up.
  • Safety and trust consequences.
  • Groups focusing only on the final error instead of earlier communication failures.

Debrief / listen for

  • Where did the breakdown begin?
  • What consequence mattered most and why?
  • What could have prevented the issue earlier?

Transition

The case shows that communication is tied directly to organizational goals.

Preview learner-facing instructions
  1. Open The Misrouted Train Incident in your eWorkbook.
  2. Read the case and discuss all four questions with your group.
  3. Capture short notes in your own eWorkbook.
  4. Your group will report one communication breakdown and one consequence when you return.
Pairs or table groups

Choose the Channel

Give participants a fast applied decision point so channel choice is not only lecture.

10 min
Open Participant eWorkbook Participant section: Choose the Channel

Instructor flow

  1. Tell participants there is usually a defensible answer, but the reason matters more than the label.
  2. Give pairs or table groups 4 minutes to decide.
  3. Debrief each scenario quickly.
  4. Reinforce that the best channel is the one most likely to create accurate shared meaning.

Watch for

  • Using written messages to avoid tension.
  • Calling meetings for simple documentation.
  • Missing written follow-up after verbal decisions.

Debrief / listen for

  • Which scenario was most difficult to decide and why?
  • Where do people in your workplace overuse email or chat?

Transition

Next, we will talk about AI as a support tool. It can help draft messages, but it does not own the communication outcome.

Preview learner-facing instructions
  1. Open Choose the Channel in your eWorkbook.
  2. For each scenario, choose verbal, written, or combined.
  3. Explain the factor that drove your choice: urgency, complexity, emotion, documentation, or need for feedback.
  4. Be ready to defend one choice during the debrief.
Full-group visual discussion

What Do You Notice First?

Show that first impressions are based on limited information and can change when context is added.

10 min
Open Participant eWorkbook Participant section: No workbook entry required

Instructor flow

  1. Reveal the image in stages and ask what participants notice first.
  2. Do not correct early interpretations.
  3. Ask what changed as more context became visible.
  4. Connect the experience to reacting before checking context.

Watch for

  • Participants jumping from observation to judgment.
  • Treating first impressions as facts.
  • Missing how added context changes the story.

Debrief / listen for

  • What did you assume at first?
  • What changed when you noticed more context?
  • How does this happen in workplace conversations?

Transition

We will pause for lunch. When we return, we will focus on adapting communication to audience, style, and context.

Preview learner-facing instructions
  1. Look at the image as it is revealed.
  2. Notice your first interpretation without correcting it yet.
  3. As more context appears, identify what changed in your interpretation.
  4. Be ready to connect the experience to workplace communication.
Individual, then full group

Style Squad: Discover Your DISC Hero

Use a short informal assessment to help participants connect a superhero archetype to a communication style.

20 min
Open Style Squad Open Participant eWorkbook Participant section: Style Strengths and Challenges

Instructor flow

  1. Clarify that Style Squad is a learning activity, not a formal DISC assessment.
  2. Give participants about 5 minutes for the assessment.
  3. Ask them to record their result color and hero type.
  4. Debrief the results as communication preferences, not fixed labels or excuses.

Watch for

  • Participants over-identifying with one label.
  • Mixed results.
  • Comments about work context changing style.
  • Strengths being used as excuses instead of flex points.

Debrief / listen for

  • Which result did you get?
  • What part of the result felt accurate?
  • What is one flex move your result suggests?

Transition

Now we will move into style groups and look at strengths and challenges.

Preview learner-facing instructions
  1. Open Style Squad from the link in your eWorkbook.
  2. Answer quickly based on what feels most natural, not what you think is ideal.
  3. Note your result color and hero type in the eWorkbook.
  4. Be ready to share one part of the result that felt accurate and one flex move it suggests.
Style-group breakouts

Style Strengths and Challenges

Help participants identify the strengths, challenges, and needs of each communication style.

18 min
Open Participant eWorkbook Participant section: Style Strengths and Challenges

Instructor flow

  1. Group participants by result color or the style they most identify with.
  2. If a group is too small, combine nearby styles or let participants self-select.
  3. Give groups 8 minutes to answer the three questions.
  4. Ask each group to report one strength, one challenge, and one wish.
  5. Debrief the idea that a strength can become a weakness when overused.

Watch for

  • Dominance wanting speed and clarity.
  • Influence wanting discussion and energy.
  • Steadiness wanting stability and impact on people.
  • Conscientiousness wanting accuracy and detail.
  • Judging styles instead of identifying preferences.

Debrief / listen for

  • What is helpful about your style?
  • What becomes difficult when that strength is overused?
  • What do you want other styles to understand?

Transition

Once we understand style preferences, we can choose how to flex our communication.

Preview learner-facing instructions
  1. Join the style group assigned by your instructor.
  2. Use the Style Strengths and Challenges section in your eWorkbook.
  3. Discuss the three questions and prepare one response for each.
  4. Be ready to report one strength, one challenge, and one thing you wish other styles understood.
Breakout groups

Know Your Audience

Practice adapting one message for different audiences while keeping the core meaning consistent.

22 min
Open Participant eWorkbook Participant section: Know Your Audience

Instructor flow

  1. Explain that each group starts with the same facts but different receivers.
  2. Give groups 10 minutes to draft or discuss the three adaptations.
  3. Save about 10 minutes for report-out.
  4. Ask what changed and what stayed the same. Facts should stay consistent while framing and emphasis may change.

Watch for

  • Simply shortening or lengthening the same message.
  • Changing facts rather than adapting framing.
  • Missing channel choice or next step.
  • Using jargon with the new employee.
  • Ignoring the experienced employee’s frustration.

Debrief / listen for

  • What did you change for each audience?
  • What stayed consistent across versions?
  • Which audience was hardest to write for?

Transition

Even after we adapt a message, conversations can shift in real time.

Preview learner-facing instructions
  1. Open Know Your Audience in your eWorkbook.
  2. Review the work-order reporting scenario and the three receiver profiles.
  3. You have 10 minutes to draft three versions of the same message.
  4. Keep the facts consistent, but adapt tone, detail, channel, emphasis, and next step.
  5. Choose one version your group is prepared to share.
Individual, then pairs

Turn It Around

Practice converting defensive language into clear, assertive language before the break.

12 min
Open Participant eWorkbook Participant section: Turn It Around

Instructor flow

  1. Frame the task as practice, not script-writing.
  2. Give 3 minutes for individual work and 2 minutes for partner comparison.
  3. Ask pairs to improve one response together.
  4. Debrief with one example from each row.
  5. Refine any response that still sounds blaming, sarcastic, or vague.

Watch for

  • Blame language.
  • Global words such as always or never.
  • No next action.
  • Sarcastic tone.
  • Too much explanation.

Debrief / listen for

  • Which defensive response was hardest to rewrite?
  • What wording helped the response sound firm without sounding aggressive?

Transition

We will take our afternoon break here. When we return, we will shift from speaking clearly to listening accurately.

Preview learner-facing instructions
  1. Open Turn It Around in your eWorkbook.
  2. Rewrite each defensive response using clear, direct, respectful language.
  3. Work independently first, then compare one response with a partner.
  4. Use the pattern: acknowledge emotion when needed, name the fact, and move to the next action.
Full-group visual discussion

Listening Lab Debrief

Compare weak and stronger listening behaviors before participants practice the skills themselves.

14 min
Open Participant eWorkbook Participant section: No workbook entry required

Instructor flow

  1. Use the first listening example to surface missed emotion, quick-fix behavior, and weak confirmation.
  2. After teaching the listening elements, show the stronger version.
  3. Ask participants to identify paraphrasing, acknowledgment, and clarifying questions.
  4. Emphasize that the problem may not be solved immediately, but the conversation becomes more accurate.

Watch for

  • Jumping to personality judgments.
  • Assuming advice equals listening.
  • Missing the value of acknowledgment and clarification.

Debrief / listen for

  • What did the listener miss in the first example?
  • What changed in the stronger example?
  • Which listening behavior made the biggest difference?

Transition

Good listeners also notice what is missing or implied, not only what is said.

Preview learner-facing instructions
  1. Compare the two listening examples shown on screen.
  2. Focus on observable behaviors rather than judging the people.
  3. Identify what the weaker listener missed and what the stronger listener did differently.
  4. Be ready to name the behavior that changed the conversation most.
Pairs or small breakouts

Active Listening Practice

Practice active listening skills in a structured way.

15 min
Open Participant eWorkbook Participant section: Active Listening Practice

Instructor flow

  1. Set the rule clearly: the listener is not solving the problem.
  2. Pair participants or create small groups.
  3. Give about 4 minutes for the first round and switch roles if time allows.
  4. Debrief what felt natural and what felt awkward.

Watch for

  • Participants trying to solve too quickly.
  • Paraphrasing sounding mechanical at first.
  • Skipping reflection of feelings.
  • Questions that turn into advice.

Debrief / listen for

  • What listening behavior felt most natural?
  • What was harder than expected?
  • What helped the speaker feel heard?

Transition

Listening helps with understanding. We also need to confirm instructions clearly.

Preview learner-facing instructions
  1. Open Active Listening Practice in your eWorkbook.
  2. One person shares a low-risk workplace frustration or uses a prepared scenario.
  3. The listener uses affirming contact, paraphrasing, reflecting feelings, and at least one clarifying question.
  4. Do not solve the problem. Your goal is to help the speaker feel heard and understood.
  5. Your instructor will tell you when to switch roles.
Individual, then full group

Closing the Loop

Practice confirmation questions that reveal whether the message was understood.

5 min
Open Participant eWorkbook Participant section: Closing the Loop

Instructor flow

  1. Explain why “Do you understand?” often produces a false yes.
  2. Point to the stronger speaker and receiver examples on screen.
  3. Ask participants to rewrite one weak confirmation question.
  4. Invite a few examples and refine them as needed.

Watch for

  • Questions that can still be answered yes or no.
  • Wording that sounds like a test.
  • Overly complex confirmation questions.

Debrief / listen for

  • What is one question you can use to confirm understanding without sounding like you are testing someone?

Transition

The final communication tool today is a simple structure for feedback.

Preview learner-facing instructions
  1. Open Closing the Loop in your eWorkbook.
  2. Replace “Do you understand?” with a question that requires the receiver to explain what they heard or what happens next.
  3. Record your version.
  4. Be ready to share one strong example.
Individual

Action Plan

Move participants from course concepts to specific communication behaviors they will apply after class.

3 min
Open Participant eWorkbook Participant section: Action Plan

Instructor flow

  1. Give participants quiet time to select two behaviors from the day.
  2. Encourage specificity and observable actions.
  3. If time allows, invite one commitment in chat or with a partner.

Watch for

  • Broad goals instead of behaviors.
  • No workplace situation or audience.
  • No first step.

Debrief / listen for

  • If time allows, ask: What is one behavior you will use first?

Transition

Let’s close by reviewing the major takeaways and resources.

Preview learner-facing instructions
  1. Open Action Plan in your eWorkbook.
  2. Choose two specific communication habits you will change or use when you return to work.
  3. Name the behavior, situation, audience, and first step.
  4. Avoid broad goals such as “communicate better.”
